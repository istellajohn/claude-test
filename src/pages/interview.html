Nobody Looks Like Themselves at Noon

A cinematographer who has spent twenty years lighting faces in the dark, on why the face arrives before the story, why flattering light is usually a lie, and what an audience decides in the first second of a close-up.

We met at dusk, which was her choice. For twenty years she has decided what audiences feel about a face before a single line of dialogue reaches them: on independent features, on music videos shot in one night, on documentaries whose subjects never agreed to be beautiful. She works mostly in low light and mostly with one lamp.

We wanted to ask her the question VALENCE asks of every brand and every founder, the one she answers professionally several times a day. What does a person look like before anyone explains them?

VALENCE

You told us to come at dusk. Was that for the light, or for us?

DOP

Both. At this hour everyone looks slightly more like themselves. The sun is low and it comes from the side, and side light gives a face its shape. At noon the light falls straight down. It fills the eye sockets with shadow, flattens the cheeks, and everybody starts to resemble their passport photograph.

Nobody looks like themselves at noon. I have believed that for twenty years and nobody has proved me wrong yet.

VALENCE

What do you mean by “like themselves”?

DOP

The version their friends would recognise. When someone you love walks into a room, you are not reading their features one at a time. You are seeing an arrangement: how the brow sits over the eyes, how much of the jaw catches the light, the small hollow under the cheekbone that changes just before they laugh. Light either reveals that arrangement or erases it. My job is to find the light that reveals it, which is rarely the most flattering light.

VALENCE

Flattering and revealing are different things?

DOP

Completely. Flattering light is soft and frontal and fills every crease. It is the light of beauty campaigns, and it works by removing information. You look younger, and you look like everyone else in the campaign.

Revealing light keeps some of the shadow. It lets you see the decisions a face has made. Audiences trust it more, though they would never put it that way. They lean in.

VALENCE

How quickly does an audience make up its mind about a face?

DOP

Before they know they are doing it. The cut lands, the face is there, and a verdict arrives in the first second: trustworthy, dangerous, tired, lying. Years after I had learnt this in the edit suite, I read about a study at Princeton where people judged how trustworthy a stranger was from a face shown for a tenth of a second.1 Giving them longer mostly made them more confident in the same judgement. It rarely changed it.

1Janine Willis and Alexander Todorov, “First Impressions: Making Up Your Mind After a 100-Ms Exposure to a Face”, Psychological Science, 2006.

VALENCE

Which means the story has to fight the first frame.

DOP

Or ride it. A director once needed the audience to trust a man for forty minutes before they discovered what he had done. If I had lit him like a villain, the twist would have died before the titles. So I lit him the way you light a kind uncle, warm and low, from the left, which turned out to be his bad side. Nobody noticed. The second time people watched the film, everybody noticed.

she drew the angle on a napkin: forty degrees left, just above the eyeline
VALENCE

That sounds like perception management. Does it ever feel dishonest?

DOP

All framing is a decision about what you are allowed to see. Every photograph of a politician, every founder on a magazine cover: somebody chose where the light came from. Deciding is unavoidable. The dishonest part is pretending nobody decided. The honest version of my job is to choose the light that serves what is true about the person, and to know which truth the film needs right now.

VALENCE

Can you light something that isn’t true?

DOP

For a while. Light can make a weak face look strong for a scene, sometimes for a whole film. It cannot do it for a career. Over time the face does what it does and the audience catches up. I have watched actors lit like stars for three films in a row, and then the audience quietly stopped believing it. The light had run ahead of the person, and there was nothing behind it to arrive.

VALENCE

And the opposite? Someone whose substance is ahead of how they are seen?

DOP

That describes most of the people I have loved working with. Character actors who spent fifteen years as the second policeman. You give them a real close-up, side light, one lamp, and the whole crew goes quiet at once, because everyone on set realises in the same second that this face has been carrying something nobody asked about.

The audience usually needs two or three films to catch up. It is like starlight. By the time it reaches you, the thing that sent it has moved on.

VALENCE

Why one lamp?

DOP

Because every extra light is an extra opinion. Add a second lamp and the face starts negotiating with itself. One source gives it a direction: a side it is lit from and a side it keeps. People are more interesting when you can tell where the light is coming from. So are brands, I imagine, which I assume is why you are here.

VALENCE

It is. What do companies get wrong about being seen?

DOP

They light themselves from the front. Every value on the website, every photograph bright and even, no shadow anywhere. It is the noon problem: you cannot find the shape of the thing. I would rather a company showed me what it has decided not to show me.

VALENCE

You work mostly at night. What does the dark give you?

DOP

Permission to choose. In daylight the world is already lit and you are only correcting it. At night nothing exists until you light it, so the first lamp you switch on is a statement about what matters in the frame.

I think that is why night scenes feel truthful to people, even though they are the most constructed thing we make. The darkness tells the audience that somebody chose, and people trust a choice more than an accident.

VALENCE

Is there a face you have never managed to light?

DOP

My father’s. I tried once, for a small personal film. Every setup made him look like a stranger, or like a photograph of himself. When you know someone completely, no single direction of light is enough, because in your memory you are seeing all of their sides at once. The camera only ever gets one.

VALENCE

What do you notice first when you meet someone?

DOP

Where the light falls on them in the room, before their clothes or whether they are smiling. Then I wonder whether they chose that spot. People who stand in good light without thinking about it are usually comfortable being seen. People who keep a window behind them are usually hiding something, very often only tiredness.

she moved her chair here, toward the window
VALENCE

What should an audience leave a film with?

DOP

One face they cannot stop seeing. The plot leaves within a week. Ask anyone about a film they saw ten years ago and they will describe a face in a particular light long before they remember what happened to it.

The three

The questions we ask everyone, last

  1. 01

    What do people feel about you in the first second?

    That I’m not listening. I am. I’m looking at where the light is.

  2. 02

    What will survive once the details are forgotten?

    A few close-ups. Maybe three. That would be a good career.

  3. 03

    What of that turns into trust?

    That I have never lit anyone to look like somebody else.
