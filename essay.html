You Are Looking at Old Light

Reputation travels like starlight. By the time it reaches anyone, the source has usually moved on. On the delay between who you are and how you are seen, and what a steady pulse can do about it.

VALENCE
Published
11 September 2026

Look at the Sun, briefly and sensibly, and you are looking about eight minutes into the past.1 The light reaching your eye left its surface before you decided to look. Find the faint smudge of the Andromeda galaxy on a dark night and the delay stretches to two and a half million years.2 Nothing in the night sky is current. Every point of it is a report, filed long ago, describing a source that has since changed, moved, or in some cases stopped existing.

1Sunlight takes about 8 minutes 20 seconds to cross the average Earth–Sun distance of 149.6 million kilometres.

2Andromeda, M31, is roughly 2.5 million light years away, one of the most distant things a person can see without a telescope.

People work the same way, and so do companies. What the market knows about you was sent some time ago. It was assembled from the website you built when the business was smaller, the photograph from a conference three roles back, the way a former colleague described you to someone who now sits on a board. By the time any of it arrives, you are somewhere else.

We spend our working lives at VALENCE inside that delay. Almost everyone who comes to us describes it, in different words. A founder says the market still treats them as the junior partner. A family business says its new generation is invisible behind the name of the old one. An artist says the audience keeps asking for the album from six years ago. Their substance has moved on. Their light has not caught up.

Nobody is lying about you

It helps to be precise about what is happening, because the usual diagnosis is wrong. The usual diagnosis is that people have the wrong idea about you, and the usual cure is a louder right idea: more posts, more announcements, a rebrand that arrives all at once like a flare.

The astronomer’s view is gentler and more useful. Nobody looking at an old star is mistaken. They are seeing exactly what reached them. The information is accurate. It is late. Correcting it means sending newer light, and accepting that newer light also takes time to travel.

That changes the question. “How do we change what people think?” is a question about other minds, which you cannot reach. “What are we sending now, and how long will it take to arrive?” is a question about your own output, which you can.

The information is accurate. It is late.

Henrietta Leavitt’s ruler

In the early twentieth century, Harvard College Observatory employed a group of women to examine photographs of the night sky: glass negatives, each holding thousands of stars, measured by eye and annotated by hand.3 One of them, Henrietta Swan Leavitt, spent years on a kind of star that brightens and dims in a regular rhythm. In 1912 her finding was published. The longer such a star takes to complete its cycle, the brighter it truly is.4

3They are often called the Harvard Computers. The observatory’s collection of glass plates, around half a million of them, is still held at Harvard.

4Harvard College Observatory Circular 173, 1912. It was issued under the director’s name, Edward Pickering, with a line stating the work had been prepared by Miss Leavitt.

That rhythm became a ruler. If you know how bright something truly is, and you can see how bright it appears, you can work out how far away it is. A little over a decade later, Edwin Hubble used stars of exactly this kind to show that some faint smudges in the sky were entire galaxies far beyond our own.5 Much of what we know about the size of the universe rests on a woman noticing that a steady pulse can be trusted.

5Hubble’s measurements of these stars in Andromeda were announced at the start of 1925.

We think about her rhythm constantly. The stars she studied were believed because they were regular, and their regularity let distant observers measure their true size. A reputation works the same way. A flare, a single launch, a campaign that explodes and vanishes tells people you exist and very little about how large you are. A steady pulse of evidence over years lets them measure you properly.

Sending newer light

In practice this asks three things of anyone whose substance has outgrown its expression.

First, find out what light is currently arriving. Ask people outside the building to describe you in a sentence, and write down their exact words. You will usually find you are being described accurately as you were some years ago. That is your old light. There is no point being offended by it.

Second, decide what the new light should say, and make it specific enough to be measured. “Innovative and trusted” gives nobody anything to check. A position that names what you do, for whom, and what you refuse to do can be tested against every piece of evidence you send.

Third, pulse. Publish, speak, build and show the work at a rhythm you can keep for years rather than weeks. Each piece of evidence is small. The rhythm is what makes it legible. Audiences, like astronomers, believe the things that keep arriving.

The person across the table

There is a quieter version of this for ordinary life. Whoever you are speaking to, you are looking at old light. The colleague who disappointed you once, the friend who was difficult at twenty-five, the relative you still describe with a story from 2009. Some of what reaches you is current. Much of it left them years ago.

We find this useful when we interview people for this journal. A conversation is one of the few instruments that shortens the delay. For an hour or two, the light arrives almost as it leaves, and the person in front of you is closer to who they are now than anything written about them.

That is what we try to publish: people as close to their current light as we can get them, written down before it travels.
